                   1. Introduction
                   After more than five decades of human presence confined to low‐Earth orbit, several space agencies (e.g., from
                   the United States, Europe, India, or China) have initiated exploratory endeavors to the Moon (e.g., Artemis III
                   Science Definition Team, 2020; Carpenter et al., 2012; Hu et al., 2023; Kanu et al., 2024). While the Apollo
                   human exploration program targeted the nearside equatorial region of the Moon, current human exploration
                   programs are focusing on south polar destinations. The enormous scientific interest in the south polar region is
                   linked to its unexplored terrain, its proximity to the ancient South Pole‐Aitken (SPA) basin, potential volatile
                   resources in permanently shadowed regions (PSRs), and the presence of regions that receive sunlight for most of
                   the lunar year (e.g., NASA, 2022b).
                   The NASA‐led Artemis program aims to land a new generation of lunar astronauts on the Moon with the Artemis
                   III mission (NASA, 2020a, 2022a, 2024). In August 2022, NASA released a list of 13 candidate landing regions
                   (CLRs; NASA, 2022a) within the initial Artemis exploration zone (AEZ; >84°S) that were narrowed down to
                   nine CLRs two years later (NASA, 2024; Figure 1). This study focuses on the identification of candidate landing
                   sites (CLSs) within these regions that fulfill the landing requirements for the Human Landing System (HLS), such
                   as having surface slopes of less than 8° (NASA, 2019). Additionally, we assess the temporal variation in illu-
                   mination conditions at these landing sites that limits their availability depending on the launch dates of the
                   Artemis missions. For each CLS, we assess how the simultaneous visibility of the Sun and Earth constrains the
                   availability of landing sites for future exploration throughout the midsummer periods from 2025 to 2032, a
                   reasonable period for the Artemis landings (NASA, 2020b). The coldest PSRs are considered to have the highest
                   potential for trapping and preserving volatile compounds, including water ice, due to their extremely low and
                   stable temperatures (e.g., Vasavada et al., 1999; Watson et al., 1961; Williams et al., 2019). In contrast, the oldest

                   PSRs may have accumulated volatiles over extended geologic timescales, potentially preserving a longer and

                   atiles, a key science objective outlined in the Scientific Context for the Exploration of the Moon (National
                   Research Council, 2007) and recommended in the Decadal Strategy for Planetary Science and Astrobiology
                   (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2023).


                   2. Data and Methodology
                   2.1. Data sets

                   To initially assess the Artemis CLRs, we used image data from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter's (LRO)
                   Wide Angle Camera with a spatial resolution of 100 m/pixel (Robinson et al., 2010), and the Narrow Angle
                   Camera (NAC) south polar mosaics, which have a resolution of ∼1 m/pixel (Archinal et al., 2023; Wagner
                   et al., 2015). We further incorporated LRO Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA) digital elevation models
                   (LDEM) at a resolution of 20 m/pixel (Barker et al., 2023; Mazarico et al., 2011; Smith et al., 2010) to
                   provide topographic context. We utilized these DEMs to (a) generate artificially illuminated hillshade maps at
                   varying azimuths to minimize potential biases caused by the challenging and variable illumination conditions
                   in the south polar region and (b) identify the location and extent of PSRs (Barker et al., 2023). Furthermore,
                   we used LOLA Reduced Data Record data (LOLA RDR; e.g., Mazarico et al., 2012; Gläser et al., 2014) for
                   diffusion modeling to ensure that topographically accurate crater shapes were used as model input. LOLA
                   RDR tracks are available in the Planetary Data System (Lunar Orbital Data Explorer–Tools) and provide
                   individual altimetry readings that sample ∼5 m spots with ∼10 cm precision along the spacecraft's orbit track
                   (Smith et al., 2010). South polar temperature maps with a spatial resolution of 240 m/pixel (Williams
                   et al., 2019) were obtained from the Diviner Lunar Radiometer Experiment, which measures the Moon's
                   infrared emissions (Paige, Foote, et al., 2010; Williams et al., 2019). We used the abovementioned data sets to
                   study the thermal conditions within PSRs, identify the coldest ones within an accessible distance of potential
                   landing sites, and ultimately assess their potential for preserving volatiles.


                   2.2. Identification of Candidate Landing Sites
                   To identify potential landing sites within the Artemis CLRs, technical requirements for landing such as slope and
                   illumination must be taken into account. The HLS has been tentatively selected to carry the Artemis III astronauts
                   to the lunar surface. As defined for the Artemis III HLS (NASA, 2019), the landing site slope “shall provide a
                   vertical orientation of 0–8° (threshold) and 0–5° (goal).” Given the HLS landing accuracy of 100 m (3σ) of a
                   target landing site (NASA, 2019), the selected landing site should be at least 100 m away from slopes steeper than
                   8°. If the HLS is capable of adjusting its vertical tilt, it may tolerate steeper surface slopes, which would increase
                   the number of viable landing sites. However, since this capability is unknown, our analysis is limited to slopes
                   between 0 and 8°, following Kring et al. (2023).
                   We generated slope maps for each of the CLRs from the 20 m/pixel resolution LDEM (Barker et al., 2023) and
                   rejected all areas that have surfaces steeper than 8°. Considering the Artemis III HLS landing accuracy of 100 m
                   (NASA, 2019), we buffered the 0–8° slope areas by − 100 m, identifying suitable areas for landing that meet the
                   HLS requirements of having gentle slopes (less than 8°) and being at least 100 m away from steeper slopes. Based
                   on this, we manually selected representative CLSs that all have shallow slopes (<5°) and, if present, are in or near
                   highly illuminated areas. The number and location of the representative landing sites chosen for each Artemis
                   CLR are intended to reflect the spatial variability of suitable landing areas. As illumination conditions are an
                   additional critical constraint for landing and exploration activities, we modeled their temporal variation in the next
                   step (Section 2.3).

                   2.3. Illumination Modeling
                   The low angle of solar incidence and the topography of the south polar region control the illumination conditions
                   in the AEZ in a much more complex pattern than the Apollo astronauts experienced in the equatorial region.
                   Therefore, illumination conditions play a critical role in the selection of the landing site, as they can affect the
                   landing approach and surface operations (e.g., Eppler, 1991; Iqbal et al., 2024; E. M. Jones, 1995; Kring
                   et al., 2025). We investigated the illumination conditions for a specific time period rather than using the averaged
                   Sun visibility for a lunar year (e.g., Mazarico et al., 2011). Because the Moon's rotational axis is tilted by 1.59°
                   with respect to the ecliptic plane (Vasavada et al., 2012), it experiences seasons that significantly affect the
                   illumination conditions near the poles. If the point where the Sun is perpendicular to the surface, referred to as the
                   subsolar latitude, is in the southern hemisphere, it is defined as southern summer, and vice versa. Since the best
                   illumination conditions for the south polar region are during southern summer, we calculated when midsummer
                   (subsolar latitude ≤ − 1.5°; Mahanti et al., 2024) occurs in the southern hemisphere from 2025 to 2032 using JPL
                   Planetary and Lunar Ephemeris data “DE441” (Park et al., 2021).
                   Since the Artemis III crew will be on the lunar surface for 6.5 days (NASA, 2019), it may be preferable to
                   maintain illumination throughout the duration of the mission. Therefore, we modeled the illumination conditions
                   utilizing the “Lunar Quickmap TerrainShadows” software developed by the Applied Coherent Technology
                   Corporation (ACT) in 2D cartographic mode with 60 m/pixel spatial resolution and 12‐hr steps. We considered a
                   surface height offset of two m, as suggested for the first generation of vertically deployed solar panels near the
                   south pole (Ross et al., 2023), to assess solar power availability for surface missions. Due to the combination of
                   low subsolar latitude (i.e., the Sun is visibly very close to the horizon) and topographically complex terrain in the
                   south polar region, with elevation changes up to 10 km, a shadow model with a 150 km maximum distance was
                   employed to account for the light rays that get intercepted by high topography far away from the CLS.
                   Being in sight of Earth can enable direct‐to‐Earth (DTE) communication and may be an important constraint for
                   early south polar landing missions without an orbiting relay satellite. Therefore, we calculated (a) solar illumi-
                   nation and (b) simultaneous Sun and Earth visibility for each Artemis CLR and each midsummer period from
                   2025 to 2032. To extract the maximum number of days with consecutive illumination, we identified consecutive
                   sunlit days (non‐zero values) for each pixel of the illumination conditions in 12‐hr steps generated before. For
                   each pixel, our output contains the maximum number of days with consecutive (a) Sun visibility and (b) combined
                   Sun and Earth visibility. Finally, we retrieved the number of consecutive illuminated days for all CLSs for the
                   midsummer periods from 2025 until 2032 (see Section 4).


                   2.4. Diffusion Modeling
                   Sampling and analyzing lunar volatiles in PSRs are key scientific objectives for future south polar landing
                   missions and a major step toward in situ resource utilization (ISRU; National Research Council, 2007). Most of
                   the identified present‐day PSRs in the lunar polar regions are within small impact craters (Bussey et al., 1999,
                   2010; Kumari et al., 2022), and under constant solar inclination, it can be assumed that the age of the host crater is
                   the maximum age of the PSR within it. Because older PSRs were potentially able to accumulate larger amounts of
                   volatiles than younger ones, determining the age of the host crater, and thus, the age of the PSR is critical for
                   identifying the oldest PSRs that lie within the ∼2 km exploration zone around each CLS.

                   We used a topographic diffusion model to determine the age of the PSR‐hosting craters, as other techniques, such
                   as performing crater size‐frequency distribution measurements, cannot confidently be used to derive ages for
                   impact craters <1 km in diameter that are prevalent across the Artemis CLRs (Neukum et al., 2001; van der
                   Bogert et al., 2025). However, the diffusion model can only be applied to small craters that were not extensively
                   modified by erosion or superposition (Du et al., 2019; Fassett & Thomson, 2014). For this reason, the craters of
                   interest were carefully chosen to avoid those significantly modified by overlapping impacts and ejecta infilling.
                   Pervasive erosion is caused by a combination of persistent impact‐derived erosion of hillslopes, mass wasting
                   triggered by impact‐induced seismicity, and thermal contraction‐relaxation cycles operating within the uppermost
                   lunar surface (e.g., Craddock & Howard, 2000; Fassett & Thomson, 2014; Molaro & Byrne, 2012; Richardson
                   et al., 2005; Schultz & Gault, 1975; Soderblom, 1970). The net effect of these processes is the mobilization and
                   transport of regolith along elevation gradients, eroding regions of higher elevation (such as crater rim crests) and

                   The diffusion of craters with a range of initial diameters was modeled at a fixed time‐step over a timeframe of
                   3.6 Ga or until the diffused crater d/D falls below 0.04 (e.g., Basilevsky et al., 2014; Fassett et al., 2022; Mahanti

                   To accurately measure the shape of the PSR‐hosting craters, we used LOLA RDR tracks (e.g., Gläser et al., 2014;
                   Mazarico et al., 2012), which provide individual altimetry readings along the spacecraft's orbit track without
                   interpolating the data (i.e., for generating DEMs). We then applied local slope corrections to the acquired
                   elevation profiles, because the diffusion model assumes craters formed on flat surfaces, rather than the complex
                   background slopes in the south polar region. We considered three types of local slope corrections: (a) craters on
                   topographic ridges (hilltops) were detrended using quadratic fitting; (b) craters on slopes were detrended using
                   linear fitting; and (c) craters on nearly flat surfaces were normalized by mean crater elevation. Next, we compared
                   the preprocessed crater shapes derived from the LOLA tracks to the modeled crater shapes to find the modeled
                   degradation state that best approximates the current topographic state of the PSR‐hosting crater. The time the
                   modeled crater takes to erode from the initial shape to the current shape is considered to be the age of the crater,
                   and thus, an upper bound age of the PSR hosted in it (see Section 6.1).

                                        3. Candidate Landing Sites
                                        The sizes of the areas that fulfill the slope and landing accuracy requirements for landing vary significantly among
                                        the 16 Artemis CLRs (Table 1). The largest suitable landing area is in the Mons Mouton Plateau (MMP) CLR,
                                        which is not surprising as this is by far the largest CLR and, as the name “plateau” implies, a relatively flat surface
                                        (Figure 1). Correlating total CLR size with the respective area that meets the HLS requirements, the Slater Plain
                                        (SP) region emerges as having the highest fractional area suitable for landing (Figure 2), followed by Nobile Rim
                                        2 (NR2) and Faustini Rim A (Table 1). The smallest suitable landing areas are located in Connecting Ridge
                                        Extension (CRE), de Gerlache Rim 1 (dGR1), MMA, and PCB with only a few square kilometers and less than
                                        2.5% of the total CLR area satisfying the constraints for landing (Table 1).
                                        In total, we identified 130 representative CLSs (Table 1) within all 16 Artemis CLRs proposed by NASA (2022a,
                                        2024). All of those CLSs represent the spatial distribution of suitable landing areas (Figure 2) and fulfill the
                                        prerequisites defined for the Artemis HLS (NASA, 2019), such as slopes <8° and at least 100 m distance to slopes
                                        steeper than 8°. In fact, 125 of the 130 CLSs (∼96%) were at slopes shallower than 5°. However, it is important to
                                        note that small features, such as young craters or boulders, may have steeper slopes that cannot be resolved at the
                                        20 m/pixel spatial resolution of the DEM. Additional 1‐ to 2‐m scale NAC image analysis should be conducted to
                                        evaluate those hazards once the list of potential CLSs has been descoped further.
                                        Because Artemis astronauts will be able to explore up to ∼2 km from the selected landing site (NASA, 2019), we
                                        prioritized representative CLSs with PSRs within that exploration radius (Figure 2) to support the key science
                                        objectives of sampling and understanding volatiles (e.g., National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and
                                        Medicine, 2023; National Research Council, 2007). The CLSs outlined here include potential Artemis landing

                   6. Identifying the Oldest Accessible Permanently Shadowed Regions
                   PSR age determination is needed to identify the oldest PSRs that may have accumulated volatiles over extended
                   geologic timescales, potentially preserving a longer and more complete record of volatile delivery and retention
                   processes on the Moon (e.g., Arnold, 1979; M. A. Siegler et al., 2011; M. Siegler et al., 2015; Watson et al., 1961).
                   We inferred PSR‐hosting crater ages from the topographic diffusion model (see Section 2.4), which provides the
                   maximum age estimate for the PSR located inside the crater.

                   6.1. Age Estimates for PSR‐Hosting Craters

                   We focused our diffusion model‐based age estimates for PSR‐hosting craters on the four Artemis CLRs that are the
                   closest to the south pole: CR, CRE, dGR1, and PNS. In total, we modeled the ages of 115 craters that lie within
                   ∼2 km radius of the identified CLSs in these four regions and host accessible PSRs. The craters range in diameters
                   from ∼150 m to ∼1,400 m, with an average diameter of ∼325 m—comparable to terrestrial nuclear test site craters
                   in Nevada (USA), such as Sedan (390 m diameter) and Schooner (295 m diameter) craters, which were used for
                   astronaut training (Kring, 2023). We modeled the erosion of these craters over time using the topographic diffusion
                   model to estimate their ages (see Section 2.4). Of the 115 PSR‐hosting craters studied, only two are younger than
                   100 Ma: a 27 ± 14 Ma old crater with 150 m diameter near CLS CR05 and a 49 ± 28 Ma old crater with 360 m
                   diameter (Figure 7a) on the rim of a larger, degraded impact structure near landing site CR03. The latter is com-
                   parable in diameter and age to Cone crater (Apollo 14), which measures 330 m in diameter and is ∼39 Ma old
                   (Hiesinger et al., 2015). However, due to steep crater wall slopes (>15°), the ∼27,000 m2 PSR within the
                   49 ± 28 Ma old crater can only be accessed from the eastern rim, where the slopes are less than 12°. The oldest PSR‐
                   hosting crater in the study is located in the PNS region (near CLS PNS03) and has a model age of 3.75 ± 0.04 Ga
                   (Figure 7c). This heavily eroded, ∼895 m diameter crater has slopes that never exceed 10°, making its ∼96,500 m2
                   PSR on the crater floor accessible for future surface missions. Although the diffusion model suggests a wide range
                   of crater ages, the median age is 876 Ma, and 85% of the studied craters are younger than 1,500 Ma (Figure 7d). Only
                   15% of PSR‐hosting craters, typically those with larger diameters, are older than 1.5 Ga.
                   The CLSs CR05, PNS03, and 007 (both in the PNS region) may provide access to a variety of craters and PSR ages
                   (Figure 8). These sites may have accumulated volatiles for over 2.5 Ga. For instance, landing site CR05 has 15 PSR‐
                   hosting craters of different ages, enabling the sampling of several PSRs in different locations to help illuminate our
                   understanding of their thermal stability, and the lateral and vertical distribution of lunar polar volatiles (Science Goal
                   4a; National Research Council, 2007). The oldest (>2 Ga) PSR‐hosting craters—which have the greatest potential to
                   have preserved a complete record of volatile storage, transport, and loss processes (e.g., Arnold, 1979; Siegler
                   et al., 2011, 2015; Watson et al., 1961)—are located in 001, CR02, CR05, CR06, dGR103, 007, PNS02, and PNS03
                   (Figure 8). To study volatile delivery and retention processes on the Moon (Science Goal 4c; National Research
                   Council, 2007) that were active in the last third of its history, CLSs in the CRE region should be considered, as they
                   host younger PSRs, less than ∼1.5 Ga in age (Figure 8). Candidate site dGR2_K2023 has the smallest crater dia-
                   meter (∼200 m) and PSR age range (<500 Ma). The mean age of PSR‐hosting craters is statistically highest in PNS
                   and lowest in CRE, with varying numbers of accessible PSR‐hosting craters per CLS (Figure 8).

                   6.2. Processes Delivering and Eroding Lunar Surface Volatiles
                   Critical information for understanding and quantifying the processes that affect volatiles on the Moon is based on
                   lunar sample analysis, surface temperature measurements, laboratory experiments, or mathematical modeling,
                   and sometimes, some of these data appear contradictory to each other (Hurley et al., 2023; Kleinhenz et al., 2020).
                   Considering that, we present an upper and lower estimate of the volatile content to represent the variations in the
                   literature and the validation that still needs to be done by future missions to the lunar surface.

                   We identified volatiles (i.e., water) delivered to the lunar surface by solar wind (e.g., Arnold, 1979), microme-
                   teoroids (e.g., Hurley et al., 2017), comets (e.g., Arnold, 1979; Greenwood et al., 2011; Ong et al., 2010; Svetsov
                   & Shuvalov, 2015), and asteroids (e.g., Arnold, 1979; Barnes et al., 2016; Ong et al., 2010; Svetsov & Shuva-
                   lov, 2015) as dominant sources of water in the last ∼1.5 Ga. After being trapped in PSRs, volatiles can be released
                   and lost by plasma sputtering induced by solar wind (e.g., Farrell et al., 2013, 2015, 2019; Johnson, 1990), impact
                   vaporization by micrometeoroids (e.g., Cintala, 1992; Farrell et al., 2015, 2019), sublimation (e.g.,
                   Andreas, 2007; Arnold, 1979; Schorghofer & Taylor, 2007; Watson et al., 1961), and ballistic sedimentation
                   (e.g., Oberbeck, 1975; Tai Udovicic et al., 2023). We note that endogenic volatiles released by episodic volcanic
                   outgassing events from the Moon's interior were a major volatile source in the past, until approximately 3 billion
                   years ago (e.g., Head et al., 2020; Needham & Kring, 2017; Wilhelms et al., 1987). Younger volcanic activity,
                   though with a much smaller volume, is evident in irregular mare patches (e.g., Braden et al., 2014; Qiao
                   et al., 2020). However, we do not anticipate that the release of volatiles by irregular mare patch activity has
                   significantly contributed to the south polar volatile content because all the PSRs that were studied here are within
                   2 km of the CLSs in the AEZ, and the irregular mare patches are predominantly located within 30° of latitude of
                   the equator (Braden et al., 2014; Qiao et al., 2020). Consequently, we did not take volcanism‐derived inputs into
                   account here.
                   Solar wind is a high‐velocity flow of ions radially emitted by the Sun. As the Moon has neither a global magnetic
                   field nor a thick atmosphere to divert the flow, these ions directly impact the lunar surface (e.g., Hurley
                   et al., 2023). Assuming that the solar wind‐implanted hydrogen reduces soil FeO to liberate the oxygen needed to
                   form water, Benna et al. (2019) calculated a solar wind‐derived mass flux of 2 g/s H2O based on the abundance of
                   metallic iron in the lunar soil (Housley et al., 1973). To estimate the amount of water that was successfully
                   transported to and trapped in PSRs, we use a trapping efficiency factor of 0.1 (Schorghofer, 2014) and 0.3 (Ong
                   et al., 2010). Thus, we derive a lower estimate of ∼165 kgH2O km− 2 Ma− 1 and an upper estimate of ∼500 kgH2O
                   km− 2 Ma− 1 for the contribution of solar wind‐derived water to PSRs.

                   Micrometeoroid impacts are frequent on the Moon (e.g., Gault et al., 1974), and as a few percent of meteorite
                   mass is water (e.g., Hanner & Zolensky, 2010), they are also a polar water contributor. Hurley et al. (2017)
                   estimated a micrometeoroid water flux of 9.46 × 106 kgH2O km− 2 Ma− 1. About ∼30% of the incoming water will
                   get incorporated into impact glass (Daly & Schultz, 2018) and ∼50% of the remaining 70% will be lost to space
                   due to the high vapor temperature (Killen et al., 2018). Therefore, ∼35% of the delivered water would be free to
                   migrate to polar cold traps (Lucey et al., 2022). Of the available water, 10% (Schorghofer, 2014) or 30% (Ong
                   et al., 2010) will be successfully transported and trapped in PSRs, suggesting a micrometeoroid‐delivered water
                   influx of ∼175 kgH2O km− 2 Ma− 1 to ∼524 kgH2O km− 2 Ma− 1, respectively.
                   Asteroid and comet impacts are not constant over time, leading to a declined impact mass rate in the last billion
                   years compared to the early bombardment history of the Moon (e.g., Stoffler et al., 2006). Mathematical modeling
                   by Ong et al. (2010) found that ∼6.5% of the water mass delivered to the Moon by comets is retained, which is
                   equivalent to 1.2 × 1010 to 3.9 × 1011 metric tons of water delivered over the last billion years. Applying the
                   effects of migration and survival for water molecules (Butler, 1997; Crider & Vondrak, 2003), ∼1.1% of the total
                   water mass delivered to the Moon can get trapped in PSRs (Ong et al., 2010). Based on these calculations, we
                   estimate a water influx of 3.4–113 kgH2O km− 2 Ma− 1 into south polar PSRs from cometary impacts. Furthermore,
                   Ong et al. (2010) estimated that ∼16.5% of the asteroidal mass impacting the Moon is retained. Estimates of
                   asteroid water content vary by types: carbonaceous chondrites can have up to 20% water (Baker, 1971), whereas
                   ordinary chondrites rarely contain water (Mason, 1972). Water accounts for ∼2.2% of the mass of impacting
                   asteroids, assuming that one‐third of them are C‐type asteroids (Shoemaker et al., 1990) and two‐thirds of C‐type
                   asteroids contain water (T. D. Jones et al., 1990), and they have an average water content of 10% by weight
                   (typical for CM meteorites; Rivkin et al., 2003). Using impactor fluxes from Shoemaker et al. (1990) and Werner
                   et al. (2002) and the average water content of 2.2 wt.%, Ong et al. (2010) calculated that 2.5 × 1015 kg of water

                   Plasma sputtering by solar wind is primarily independent of the surface temperature (Farrell et al., 2019) and can
                   effectively erode surface volatiles in the uppermost few nanometers (Johnson, 1990). The ice composition ratio of
                   the ice‐regolith mixture controls the sputtering rate of water, yielding a water release rate of 8 × 107 H2O m− 2 s− 1
                   for a 1 wt.% ice‐regolith mixture (Farrell et al., 2019), equivalent to ∼75.6 kgH2O km− 2 Ma− 1. Most of the released
                   water molecules do not have the velocity (>2.4 km/s) necessary to escape lunar gravity and thus will land
                   elsewhere on the Moon (Farrell et al., 2013, 2015). Consequently, the estimate used here is likely a higher es-
                   timate of released water as local topography can capture liberated water molecules excavated in highly oblique
                   emission angles (Farrell et al., 2019).
                   Impact Vaporization due to micrometeoroids can continuously release surface volatiles trapped in PSRs
                   (e.g., Farrell et al., 2019). Impact vaporization only erodes surficial volatiles, as micrometeoroids only penetrate
                   <5 mm into the surface (Farrell et al., 2013) and have impact energies too low to emit large vapor volumes. Using
                   the vaporization mass flux of Cintala (1992) and a 1 wt.% icy regolith, Farrell et al. (2019) suggested a volatile
                   release rate of 2 × 108 H2O m− 2 s− 1. Therefore, we calculated that the water release rate from south polar PSRs is
                   ∼189 kgH2O km− 2 Ma− 1 assuming that the icy regolith contains 1 wt.% of ice. However, impact vaporization is a
                   lower energy process compared to sputtering and thus, even less molecules reach escape velocities (Farrell
                   et al., 2015). Consequently, our estimate represents a higher limit for the lost volatiles, as many of the emitted
                   water molecules are repeatedly available for migration and trapping in PSRs.
                   Sublimation driven by temperature can substantially release volatiles from the lunar surface (e.g., Andreas, 2007;
                   Arnold, 1979; Schorghofer & Taylor, 2007; Watson et al., 1961). If a PSR's temperature increases by 10 K from
                   110 to 120 K, the sublimation rate increases by a factor of 100 (Andreas, 2007), highlighting an extreme tem-
                   perature sensitivity (Figure S2.1 in Supporting Information S1). To infer the thermal environment of each PSR
                   within the ∼2 km exploration zones of the CLSs, we calculated the average temperature for a lunar year using
                   Diviner temperature maps from Williams et al. (2019). The sublimation rate depends on temperature, ice‐regolith
                   ratio, regolith grain size distribution, and the physical form of water ice such as pure water ice deposits on the
                   surface, ice coating regolith grains with convex ice surfaces, or ice connecting two grains with concave ice
                   surfaces (Figure S2.2 in Supporting Information S1). At 40 K, the sublimation rate of a convex or concave surface
                   (with a radius of 5 μm) versus a planar surface is ∼2.3% higher and ∼2.3% lower, respectively. At 100 K, the
                   difference in sublimation is less than 0.8% between a convex and a concave surface with respect to a planar
                   surface (Figure S2.3 in Supporting Information S1). At higher temperatures and larger radii of curvature (larger
                   grains) the difference between sublimation from a curved and planar surface decreases rapidly. For an average
                   regolith grain size of 70 μm, as sampled by Apollo 16 (Carrier et al., 1991), and a temperature of 110 K, the
                   differences in sublimation are of the order of 1.35 × 10− 5 and thus below the uncertainty introduced by the
                   Diviner temperature data. Therefore, we used the sublimation rate for planar ice surfaces to estimate the total
                   volatile content of PSRs with 1 wt.% icy regolith. See Text S2 in Supporting Information S1 for the equations and
                   a detailed description of how we calculated the sublimation rate for volatiles within PSRs.

                   Ballistic sedimentation describes the process of ejecta following ballistic trajectories at high velocities and mixing
                   ejecta material with local materials by impacting the surface, causing heating of the mixed ejecta (Ober-
                   beck, 1975). Disruption of south polar volatiles in PSRs by ballistic sedimentation was modeled by Tai Udovicic
                   et al. (2023) for craters larger than 20 km in diameter. They inferred the threshold kinetic energy for the onset of
                   ballistic sedimentation on Earth to be between 10 and 1,500 MJ/m2 (Tai Udovicic et al., 2023). Additionally, the
                   deposition of continuous ejecta is restricted to within 4 crater radii from the crater center (Melosh, 1989).
                   However, since the PSR‐hosting craters in this study are relatively young (75% of the studied craters are younger
                   than 1,332 Ma) and small (<1 km in diameter), none of them reached the kinetic threshold energy required to
                   initiate ballistic sedimentation. Larger craters in the vicinity (e.g., Spudis or Marvin craters near CLRs CR and
                   CRE) are more degraded and thus likely older than the small craters hosting PSRs close to the CLSs. Conse-
                   quently, those larger craters formed prior to the PSRs and could not have disrupted the volatile deposits.

                   6.3. Estimated Volatile Volumes
                   To estimate potential volatile abundances in south polar PSRs in kilograms (kg), we calculated the volatile
                   influx and loss rate for a given area and time period (in kg km− 2 Ma− 1) and multiplied the difference of both
                   with the modeled age of the PSR‐hosting crater (in Ma; Thaker et al., 2025). This provides information on
                   whether a PSR has the potential to trap and accumulate volatiles over time. For each CLS in the four Artemis
                   CLRs (CR, CRE, dGR1, and PNS), we estimated the cumulative volatile abundance of all PSRs within
                   craters 2 km from the landing site for which we have model ages (Figure 9). These estimates take the
                   variation in the literature on the volatile in‐ and outflux rate and temperature‐dependent sublimation rate into
                   account (see Section 6.2), providing upper and lower estimates of volatile abundance. These calculations
                   reflect volatile delivery and loss rates in the literature (Andreas, 2007; Arnold, 1979; Barnes et al., 2016;
                   Cintala, 1992; Farrell et al., 2013, 2015, 2019; Greenwood et al., 2011; Hurley et al., 2017; Johnson, 1990;
                   Oberbeck, 1975; Ong et al., 2010; Schorghofer & Taylor, 2007; Svetsov & Shuvalov, 2015; Tai Udovicic
                   et al., 2023; Watson et al., 1961), producing modeled potential volatile abundances within CLS PSRs. While
                   the calculations are not prima facie evidence for the presence of volatiles, they can be used to guide the
                   selection of landing sites that can be used to test delivery and loss rates and thus improve such calculations
                   for future mission activities.
                   Due to the limited data on the storage depth and subsurface stability of lunar volatiles, we simplified our
                   calculation by assuming that volatile reservoirs are at the PSR surface. However, if the ice is buried beneath a thin
                   regolith layer, it would be protected from plasma sputtering and impact vaporization. In addition, if the ice is not
                   exposed at the surface, an insulating layer of regolith would protect it from extreme surface temperatures,
                   significantly reducing volatile loss by sublimation. Consequently, the quantification of ice loss processes may be
                   an overestimation of volatiles released from PSRs in the study area. Therefore, all volatile estimates presented
                   here should be considered approximations showing whether PSRs have the potential to preserve trapped volatiles
                   rather than absolute values. In addition, landing and surface operations within a few kilometers of PSRs may
                   locally perturb surface thermal conditions and volatile stability, requiring consideration of environmental
                   sensitivity for future missions (Ahrens et al., 2025). To gain a more complete understanding of the processes
                   affecting lunar volatiles, ground truth verification by future exploration missions is essential. Such missions
                   would provide direct observations and measurements to validate existing models and refine our knowledge of
                   volatile behavior on the Moon.
                   Of the 27 CLSs identified in the four Artemis CLRs for which we have volatile estimates, only 14 have PSRs that
                   are cold and old enough to trap and accumulate volatiles over time. The cumulative volatile abundance of these
                   landing sites can differ significantly, ranging from 409 kg in CRE02 to nearly 644,952 kg in PNS03 (Figure 9).
                   Generally, older and/or colder PSRs have the most volatiles because they have had the most time to accumulate
                   and/or preserve volatiles, as sublimation is less effective at low temperatures (e.g., Andreas, 2007; Arnold, 1979;
                   Schorghofer & Taylor, 2007; Watson et al., 1961; see Section 6.2). The distinct correlation between average PSR
                   temperature and estimated volatile abundance suggests that temperature is the driving factor for volatile accu-
                   mulation. ISRU of polar volatiles is part of the Artemis program's “sustainable exploration” goal (NASA, 2020a).
                   As reflected in NASA's Moon to Mars strategy (NASA, 2022b), the water can be used as is or to produce oxygen
                   and hydrogen to fuel vehicles, power systems, and life support consumables. If the objective is the production of
                   10 tons of oxygen, approximately 15 tons of water per year are required (Sanders et al., 2008; Sanders &
                   Kleinhenz, 2022). However, when considering an icy regolith containing ∼1 wt.% of water, it should be noted that
                   about 1,500 truckloads of lunar regolith (each truckload equaling one metric ton) will be needed to extract 15 tons
                   of water, requiring a large, well‐organized infrastructure. Although the Artemis III mission will not rely on ISRU,
                   and will lack the necessary infrastructure (Kleinhenz & Sanders, 2023), ISRU will become increasingly important
                   for subsequent Artemis missions and other future surface exploration activities (NASA, 2022b). Most CLSs could
                   sustain the 15‐ton objective for at least one year (Figure 9). Landing sites in the CRE region could support ISRU
                   for lunar infrastructure for a maximum of 2 years (assuming the upper volatile estimate). Landing site CR05 could
                   potentially fulfill the ISRU objective for approximately 13 years under a conservative scenario based on the lower
                   volatile estimate, or ∼28 years under a more optimistic scenario based on the upper estimate. Only landing site
                   PNS03, if the presence of water is confirmed there, has the potential to meet the goal of 15 tons per year for 20 to
                   43 consecutive years.

                   7. Considerations for Future Exploration
                   We identified CLSs that meet the HLS requirements for all 16 Artemis CLRs. Additionally, we modeled the
                   illumination for the midsummer periods from 2025 to 2032 to determine the maximum number of consecutive
                   illuminated days that limits site availability depending on the launch dates. Furthermore, we investigated the
                   highest temperatures experienced by the PSRs close to the landing sites to evaluate their ability to retain
                   volatiles. For four CLRs (CR, CRE, dGR1, and PNS), we derived diffusion model ages for PSR‐hosting
                   craters accessible from the landing sites to estimate volatile abundance in these PSRs, considering pro-
                   cesses that deliver and erode surface volatiles on the Moon. We summarize the results and data presented in
                   this work, as well as their implications for the future exploration of each of the 16 Artemis CLRs in this
                   section. However, it should be noted that suggestions regarding individual sites or regions are based on the
                   distribution of potentially interesting PSRs within 2 km of HLS‐compatible landing sites and with nominal
                   <20° slopes suitable for walking EVA; they do not yet include an assessment of other risks and hazards
                   (e.g., Carton et al., 2025; Jo et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2025).

                   Mons Mouton (MMO), originally outlined as Leibnitz Beta Plateau in NASA (2022a) and renamed in
                   NASA's (2024) update, is located near the southern margin of the ∼8 km high Mons Mouton. This region overlaps
                   with the other Artemis CLRs, MMP and NR1 (Figure 1). We identified 10 CLSs in the region, most of which will
                   have suitable illumination conditions during the midsummers of 2025, 2028, 2029, and 2032. However, during
                   the remaining midsummers until 2032, significantly fewer candidate sites will receive sufficient sunlight.
                   Landing sites MMO01 and MMO02 stand out because of their favorable Sun and Earth visibility every
                   midsummer until 2032. Sites MMO07 and MMO08 offer access to PSRs in their vicinity that remain below
                   110 K year‐round and should therefore be considered potential destinations for investigating and sampling lunar
                   volatiles contained in icy regolith.

                   Mons Mouton Plateau (MMP) is the largest of the Artemis CLRs outlined by NASA (2024) for the Artemis III
                   mission (Figure 1). MMP encompasses most of the ∼8 km high Mons Mouton, a nearly flat‐topped SPA massif
                   (e.g., Bernhardt et al., 2025) that formed when structurally separated crustal blocks were uplifted to form the SPA
                   basin's rim (Wueller et al., 2025). About 12.7% or 564.8 km2 of the MMP meet the HLS requirements for landing,
                   which is more suitable landing area than of all other CLRs combined (Table 1). We detailed 11 representative
                   CLSs that are distributed across the plateau. Landing sites MMP02, MMP05, and MMP06 provide access to PSRs
                   with temperatures remaining below 110 K, while MMP03 and MMP08 are within 2 km of PSRs that stay below
                   125 K year‐round (Table 3), which may be ideal for studying the seasonal variation and temperature dependency
                   of volatile accumulation. Our illumination modeling shows that in midsummers 2025, 2028, 2029, 2030, and
                   2032, most of the 11 CLSs remain sunlit for more than 10 consecutive days, enabling extended surface explo-
                   ration. However, during midsummers 2027 and 2031, none of the representative CLSs meet the illumination
                   criteria. Nevertheless, MMP's large extent suggests that relocating the CLSs within the HLS‐compliant area could
                   still provide viable landing options in those years.

                   Nobile Rim 1 (NR1) overlaps with the other Artemis CLRs, MMO and MMP, and is located on Mons Mouton,
                   near the western rim of the Nobile crater (Figure 1; NASA, 2022a, 2024). Nine CLSs have been identified along
                   the Nobile crater rim. Candidate sites NR101 and NR103 offer consistently favorable illumination with more than
                   17 consecutive sunlit days during every midsummer until 2032. Most of the other sites will be accessible during
                   the midsummers of 2025, from 2028 to 2030, and in 2032. Although maximum summer temperatures remain

                   Nobile Rim 2 (NR2) is located at the northern rim of the Nobile crater where it intersects with an unnamed,
                   ∼55 km diameter crater (Figure 1; NASA, 2022a, 2024). We selected nine potential landing sites that represent
                   the size and distribution of the area that meets the HLS requirements for landing in the NR2 region. Our illu-
                   mination modeling indicates that none of the landing sites will be suitable for landing during midsummer 2027
                   and 2031. However, during the midsummers of 2025, 2029, 2030, and 2032, all of the CLSs will provide
                   favorable illumination. Two km2‐sized PSRs near NR204 may maintain surface temperatures below 125 K during
                   the summer season, suggesting potential volatile retention that could be targeted by future surface exploration
                   missions.
                   The Peak Mear Cabeus B (PCB) is situated between the Cabeus and Drygalski craters on a ∼6 km high
                   massif that is located about 35 km southwest of the Cabeus B crater (Figure 1; NASA, 2024). Among all 16
                   CLRs, PCB has the smallest area fraction that meets the HLS slope requirement, with only 0.5% or 2.1 km2 of
                   the total area suitable for landing (Table 1). We identified five CLSs (Figure 2b). However, none of these
                   sites offer access to nearby PSRs that maintain surface temperatures below 125 K throughout the year
                   (Table 3; Williams et al., 2019), limiting the potential to sample and analyze volatiles in the region. Our
                   illumination analyses show that in the midsummers of 2025, 2027, 2031, and 2032, most CLSs will be
                   sufficiently lit to support a 6.5‐day surface mission. In contrast, the region is entirely unavailable in
                   midsummer 2026 due to limited sunlight, and in 2028, 2029, and 2030, only one of the five CLSs meets the
                   illumination requirements for landing and traversing.
                   Peak Near Shackleton (PNS) is located between Shackleton and Slater craters (Figure 1; NASA, 2022a) in an
                   area likely altered by the Shackleton impact ∼3.4 Ga ago (Kring et al., 2021). We detailed six CLSs,
                   including previously identified (NASA, 2020a) and characterized (Kumari et al., 2022) site 007, which has
                   favorable illumination for landing in all midsummers through 2032. Although illumination varies across sites
                   and years, at least three sites remain suitable for landing each year. Landing sites PNS01 and PNS02 offer
                   access to PSRs with extremely low midsummer temperatures below 90 K, whereas sites PNS02 and PNS03
                   host some of the oldest PSR‐hosting craters in this study with model ages of 3.2 and 3.75 Ga, respectively.
                   Volatile estimates suggest substantial abundance in PSRs near the landing sites, with up to ∼645,000 kg at
                   PNS03. In particular, site PNS02 stands out as it hosts an extremely low temperature PSR as well as one of
                   the oldest PSR‐hosting craters (see Section 6.3). This results in the highest potential to retain volatiles due to
                   cold temperatures and volatile accumulation over extended geologic timescales. Therefore, the PSRs near site
                   PNS02, which have a cumulative volatile abundance of ∼150,000 kg, should be considered potential targets
                   for future missions aiming to study various volatile compounds and sample a billion years record of volatile
                   delivery and retention on the Moon.
                   Slater Plain (SP) is located ∼20 km northeast of Slater crater on the lunar farside (Figure 1; NASA, 2024).
                   Among the 16 CLRs, SP stands out with the highest proportion of terrain (19.3% or 77 km2; Table 1) that
                   meets the HLS slope requirement. We identified 11 CLSs that represent the spatial distribution of the
                   available landing area across the region (Figure 2a). CLS SP11 is within the suggested landing area of
                   Samaddar et al. (2025) that is devoid of any large boulders in Mini‐RF radar data. Four of our sites (SP01,
                   SP02, SP10, and SP11) provide access to PSRs within 2 km that remain below 110 K, offering promising
                   targets for investigating potentially volatile‐rich regolith. Two additional CLSs (SP04 and SP08; Table 3)
                   have nearby PSRs that can maintain temperatures below 125 K, making them ideal for studying volatile
                   retention. Our illumination modeling shows that during the midsummers of 2025, 2026, 2027, and 2030, most
                   CLSs will receive extended sunlight for over 10 consecutive days. However, during the midsummers of 2028,
                   2029, 2031, and 2032, only one or two CLSs will meet this threshold, which limits surface mission op-
                   portunities during those years.

                   8. Conclusions
                   Sampling and analyzing volatiles in polar PSRs were recommended (e.g., National Research Council, 2007) to
                   advance our understanding of their origin, distribution, and stability. Such missions help identify potential volatile
                   reservoirs that could be utilized for a sustained human presence on the Moon (NASA, 2020a). Because little is

                   to explore volatiles and their environment within south polar PSRs (e.g., Artemis III Science Definition
                   Team, 2020; NASA, 2022b).

                   Therefore, we analyzed the surface slopes of the 16 Artemis CLRs to identify suitable landing areas that meet
                   the HLS's requirements, such as having shallow surface slopes of less than 8° (ideally <5°) and being more
                   than 100 m away from steeper slopes (NASA, 2019). Within those suitable landing areas, we identified 130
                   representative candidate landing sites (CLSs; Figure 2; Table A1) that all have accessible PSRs within 2 km,
                   since this is the maximum distance an astronaut can explore during a walking extravehicular activity
                   (NASA, 2019).
                   To ensure that the CLSs receive sufficient sunlight for the 6.5‐day surface mission (NASA, 2019), we used an
                   illumination model deriving the maximum number of consecutive illuminated days during the midsummer pe-
                   riods from 2025 to 2032. Considering a buffer before and after the surface operation, we define favorable illu-
                   mination when the CLS is sunlit for at least 10 consecutive days. Our modeling shows that illumination conditions
                   vary significantly on a temporal and spatial scale among the Artemis CLRs. Thus, depending on the launch year,
                   different CLSs will be available for landing and surface mobility (Table A2). Additionally, our illumination
                   model suggests that there will be only minor limitations in availability when simultaneous Sun and Earth visibility
                   is required to enable direct‐to‐Earth communication (Figure 5). Consequently, we recommend selecting and
                   ranking multiple landing sites according to their availability for a planned surface mission. This provides flex-
                   ibility in launch times and ensures that backup sites are available for later mission windows. Some CLSs (e.g.,
                   CR06, CR07, and 001) have ≥16 consecutive sunlit days in all studied years and could be considered for longer
                   surface missions.
                   We identified CLSs that may provide access to the coldest and oldest PSRs by integrating Diviner temper-
                   ature data (Williams et al., 2019) with a newly developed topographic crater diffusion model (Text S1 in
                   Supporting Information S1; solely applied for the Artemis CLRs CR, CR Extension, dGR1, and PNS). We
                   inferred the PSR ages from the model ages of the PSR‐hosting craters, indicating that the majority of PSRs
                   within 2 km of the CLSs are relatively young (85% of the studied craters are younger than 1,500 Ma). To
                   estimate the potential amount of volatiles in PSRs, we examined the delivery of volatiles (i.e., water) by solar
                   wind, micrometeoroids, comets, and asteroids, which are the dominant sources of water over the past
                   ∼1.5 Ga. After volatiles are trapped in PSRs, they can be released and lost through plasma sputtering induced
                   by solar wind, impact vaporization by micrometeoroids, sublimation, and ballistic sedimentation. Although
                   quantification of those processes is still unresolved, we used a range of literature values to identify PSRs with
                   relatively higher potential to harbor volatiles. Trapping and retention of volatiles is largely controlled by
                   surface temperature and PSR age; colder (e.g., FRA04, FRA06, PNS01, PNS02, SP01, and SP10; Table 3)
                   and/or older (e.g., CR05, dGR103, 007, PNS02, and PNS03; Figure 8) PSRs have the greatest preservation
                   potential. Cumulative estimated volatile abundances suggest that some landing sites may provide access to
                   hundreds of tons of water (Figure 9), such as PNS03 (644,952 kg), CR05 (425,582 kg), and PNS02
                   (150,540 kg), which could sustain NASA's ISRU target for multiple years (Sanders et al., 2008; Sanders &
                   Kleinhenz, 2022). However, compared to the volume of an Olympic swimming pool, these volatile estimates
                   could fill it by only ∼26%, ∼17%, or ∼6%, respectively (Figure 10a).
                   Our results can be used to select south polar landing sites where astronauts can safely explore and access PSRs that
                   may contain volatiles that can be sampled to illuminate our understanding of their origin and distribution. These
                   new findings will help prepare future missions that could utilize the Moon's resources for a longer‐term human
                   presence.
